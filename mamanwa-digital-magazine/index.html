01 / TWO COMMUNITIES

Two communities, shared heritage

Two peoples,
not one story.

The Mamanwa and the Badjao/Sama Dilaut are distinct Indigenous peoples. This issue places them side by side to compare how each is documented, not to suggest they share one history or identity.

The Mamanwa primary focus

An Indigenous ethnolinguistic group mainly associated with northeastern Mindanao, especially the Caraga Region (Agusan del Norte, Surigao del Norte, Surigao del Sur), with communities also documented in Southern Leyte. Their language, Minamanwa, is Austronesian (Central Philippine) and considered endangered. They share the region with the Manobo and Higaonon, who have their own languages and identities.

The Badjao / Sama Dilaut comparative community

Associated historically with the Sulu Archipelago (including Tawi-Tawi), Basilan, and Zamboanga, with related Sama-Bajau communities in Sabah and eastern Indonesia. Many have long ties to the sea, but not all live on boats; many live in permanent coastal or urban settlements. They speak different Sama varieties (Sinama).

Names vary by place and by how people identify. “Badjao,” “Sama-Bajau,” and “Sama Dilaut” overlap in some sources but are not interchangeable everywhere, and some sources note that “Bajau” can carry negative connotations in the Philippines. This issue follows each source's own term. Locations and practices differ by community; no description here applies to every community.

LIMITED / FRAGMENTED

Population & geography

Counts are scattered across labels and years. Public settlement maps are limited for both: the team found no open GIS layer for either community. This is a research limitation, not proof that none exists.

DOCUMENTED

Research exists on language, oral literature, crafts, dance, and environmental knowledge for both, but it is spread across separate academic and institutional publications.

04 / BEYOND THE NUMBERS

Why fragmented records matter

Not every gap
is an absence.

Outdated, scattered, or hard-to-reach records can leave a community described by a single old statistic. Items below are tagged as sourced findings or as observations from the team's own review. Neither community is reducible to hardship: both have documented knowledge, skills, and community-led efforts.

01

Outdated references

The Mamanwa profile cites 1990 data; Badjao monograph figures date to 1986–1994. Age alone can mislead.

02

Terms and classification

Mamanwa sources may count ethnic population, speakers, or a combined group. Badjao data is split across three census labels, and “Badjao,” “Sama-Bajau,” and “Sama Dilaut” are not always interchangeable.

Counting and documentation

A 2015 report said boat-dwelling families were counted only when boats reached shore. A Bongao profiling report found 67% of surveyed individuals lacked a birth certificate (that survey only). Records can miss the people they describe.

05 / AN INFORMATION SYSTEM

A proposed concept

Mamanwa Digital
Heritage & Information System

This is a proposal only. No system has been built, no cultural content has been collected, and no Mamanwa community has been consulted or has approved it. Any real version would begin with community consultation and free, prior, and informed consent (FPIC).

The problem

Mamanwa information is fragmented across government pages, academic studies, and registries; it is hard to verify, often dated, and rarely shaped by the community. Intended users: students, teachers, and researchers, with Mamanwa communities holding decision-making authority and agencies such as NCIP, LGUs, and DepEd as possible partners.

What already exists

NCIP's Data Repository on Indigenous Peoples (DRIP, launched December 2025 per team research, date to verify) centralizes IP information for policy. NCCA's TALAPAMANA registers cultural properties. Neither shows that Mamanwa heritage is comprehensively documented for public discovery. The gap this proposal targets: public discovery, verification, and community-governed presentation of Mamanwa heritage. It would complement, not replace, these systems.

06 / THE IS PERSPECTIVE

Technology with responsibility

Good systems are
more than software.

Information Systems connect people, processes, data, and technology. For Indigenous heritage, a platform is only useful if it is accurate, safe, accountable, and governed with the community. Technology should support community authority, not replace it.

01

Data integrity & quality

A 1990 count and a 2020 language figure are stored with their definitions and years, never merged into one “fact.”

02

Metadata & provenance

Each record carries its author, date, source, and verification status, so a missionary database is labeled differently from a peer-reviewed study.

03

Privacy & security

No precise coordinates for sensitive places and no identifiable photos of children without consent. Personal data is also covered by the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173, to verify).

04

Access control & authorization

Four tiers, researcher requests reviewed individually, bulk downloading disabled for sensitive collections, and access logged.

05

Indigenous data sovereignty

Communities govern data about their lands and knowledge, supported by IPRA (RA 8371) and the CARE principles: Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics.

06

Ethical information governance

A written governance agreement comes before any collection; FPIC, benefit-sharing, and cultural labels such as TK Labels tell users how an item may be used.

07

Accessibility & equitable access

A design goal, not yet researched with users: phone-friendly, low-bandwidth, and available in languages such as Minamanwa and Sinama where the community wants it.

08

Responsible design & accountability

Communities can audit usage reports, revoke access, and request corrections; the central question is not “how do we digitize?” but “who gets to decide?”

07 / TAKE ACTION

Visibility with dignity

Make room for
the whole story.

A data gap is not permission to speculate. It is a reason to verify, listen, document responsibly, and respect the people the data describes.

  1. Verify before sharing. Check the original source, year, and definition.
  2. Credit creators and sources. Name photographers, authors, and communities.
  3. Seek consent before documenting sensitive knowledge or people.
  4. Respect cultural restrictions on what may be recorded or shown.
  5. Protect personal information and sensitive locations.
  6. Support community-led preservation, such as language and learning projects.
  7. Correct outdated or inaccurate information when you find it.
  8. Recognize community authority over their own heritage and data.

Unified group synthesis

Responsible information systems can make Indigenous heritage more discoverable while protecting the rights and authority of the communities represented. Our advocacy calls for verified data, respectful documentation, and technology governed by consent, not visibility at any cost.
